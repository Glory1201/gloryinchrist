Once my foundations in Islam began to crumble, I found myself in a kind of religious vacuum. I wasn’t merely questioning a few doctrines — the entire framework I had built my life upon was collapsing. For the first time, it seemed entirely plausible that religion might be nothing more than a human construct.<br><br>
Yet atheism was never an option. I knew too well that the universe was not an accident. If I was going to rebuild my faith, it had to be on truth — not tradition, fear or cultural expectation. I refused to replace one falsehood with another.<br><br>
God met me in that place of searching. Through my now husband, his family and their church, I encountered someone I had never truly known before: <strong>Jesus</strong> — Not the distant figure of my childhood, but the living God who sees, heals and restores. Through Him, I was born again.<br><br>
But I need to be clear: for a Muslim, Christianity is a hard pill to swallow. From childhood we are taught that God cannot be a Father and cannot have a Son. God must be one person, one being with one identity. Anything else violates tawhid, the absolute oneness of God. Surah 112 — Al Ikhlāṣ — is memorised by every Muslim and includes the line: <br><br>
“He does not beget, nor is He begotten” <br><br>
This forms the backbone of Islamic resistance to the Christian claim that Jesus is the Son of God. So, when I first encountered Christianity, I wasn’t just wrestling with unfamiliar ideas — I was confronting concepts my entire upbringing had trained me to reject, especially since Allah NEVER enters creation.<br><br>
What I didn’t realise was that Islam contains a concept that, on the surface, resembles the very Christian idea I was resisting. In classical Sunni theology, the Quran is the <strong>eternal, uncreated speech of God.</strong> Islamic tradition even teaches that the Quran will <strong>intercede</strong> for believers on the Day of Judgement as described in this <a href="https://islamidawahcenter.com/how-the-quran-will-intercede-for-believers/">Islamic blog post</a>.<br><br>
So, Islam presents a Quran that is eternal, uncreated and personified enough to intercede — a concept that echoes Christian discussions about God’s Word. But the similarity is only superficial. In Islam, Allah’s speech is simply one of His attributes, like His 99 names, not a distinct person.<br><br>
This raises deeper questions... If Allah is one person, alone from eternity, then his relational names — the Merciful, the Loving, the Forgiving — only become meaningful after creation appears. In other words, Allah needs creation to express these attributes. But a God who needs creation cannot be eternally complete. He cannot be eternally loving, because love requires an object. He cannot be eternally merciful, because mercy requires someone to receive it. He cannot be eternally forgiving, because forgiveness requires a sinner. <br><br>
Everything changed when I discovered Tudor Alexander’s Dance of Life podcast, especially his <a href="https://www.danceoflife.com/p/the-trinity-series">Trinity series</a>. His explanations helped me grasp the nature of the true God clearly enough to finally know Him. <br><br>
To summarise the Trinity briefly: <br><br>
<strong>God has always existed as three eternal persons — Father, Son and Holy Spirit — one divine being in a tri-personal unity. </strong> This did not begin at creation; it is who God is. <br><br>
The Son entered creation as Jesus Christ, taking on a human nature while remaining fully God. Even before His incarnation, the Son interacted with creation as the Angel of the Lord — the visible manifestation of God who speaks as God, receives worship as God, yet is distinct from God. Ancient Jews recognised this plurality and described it as the <a href="https://twopowersinheaven.com/">Two Powers in Heaven</a>. Only with the New Testament do we see the full revelation of God as Triune, revealing the Holy Spirit as the life giver who regenerates and indwells. The Father is the invisible one who draws us to the Son through the Spirit. Together they are three persons, one God — a complex unity. <br><br>
And because God exists outside space and time, He is not bound by human logic or mathematical categories that belong to creation. This is why certain aspects of Him will always remain a mystery. <br><br>
So when Muslims try to force the Trinity into a mathematical box — 1 + 1 + 1 = 3 — a better analogy is this: <strong>infinity + infinity + infinity is still infinity. </strong>Three persons, one infinite being. <br><br>
Analogies will always fall short because God exists beyond the physical. Yet consider a human being: body, mind and soul — distinct in function yet one unified being. Now imagine those aspects relating to one another as if they were three persons. Even this cannot fully capture God, but it helps us see why “three persons, one being” is not incoherent. It is certainly not comparable to a square circle, because God can be <strong>ONE in one way (being) </strong> and <strong>THREE in another (persons). </strong> <br><br>
Unlike Allah, the Christian God — Father, Son and Holy Spirit — who exists outside creation, <strong>entered creation.</strong> He stepped into time and space as Jesus Christ, fully God and fully human, two natures united in one person. Muslims often struggle to understand why this was necessary because their concept of sin is completely different. They do not view the fall in the Garden of Eden in the same way, which is why Islamic theology sees no need for a blood sacrifice for atonement. <br><br>
But in Christianity, the one God who is Father, Son and Holy Spirit is both truly just and truly good. Only God Himself was qualified to pay for our sins with the eternal blood of the person of the only begotten Son, Jesus Christ. <br><br>
But what does “begotten” mean? Many Muslims assume it implies biological generation. Even some early Christians misunderstood it as eternal subordination. Yet in Christian theology, “begotten” does not refer to biological origin or inequality. <br><br>
The Son has always been divine and fully equal with the Father. So, when Scripture calls Him “begotten”, it is not describing how He came into being. It is describing His unique and honoured place — the true High Priest and the “firstborn” in the sense of supreme authority and pre eminence. It is a title of identity and relationship. <br><br>
This misunderstanding is also why Muhammad rejected the Christian concept of the Son. In Surah 6:101 the Qur’an says: <br><br>
“How can He have a son when He has no consort?” <br><br>
This assumes Christians believe God has a wife or that the Son is produced biologically — which we do not believe. It also explains why Muhammad misunderstood the Trinity as Allah, Jesus and Mary. He did not understand who the Holy Spirit is. <br><br>
Many Muslims today repeat a similar misunderstanding when they claim Muhammad was prophesied in the Bible, often pointing to John 14:16. Yet the passage clearly refers to the Holy Spirit, not a human prophet: <br><br>
“And I will ask the Father, and he will give you another Helper, to be with you forever, even the Spirit of truth, whom the world cannot receive, because it neither sees him nor knows him. You know him, for he dwells with you and will be in you.”  - <strong>John 14:16–17 (ESV) </strong><br> <br> 
Ultimately, God will always remain a mystery — and rightly so. If He were easy to comprehend or fit neatly into human categories, He would not be a God worthy of worship. The true God is infinite, eternal and unbound by the limitations of space, time and human reasoning. <br><br>
When I explore the problem of sin more deeply, this becomes even clearer. Sin separates us from God because God is life, so stepping away from Him leads to death. Because of the fall in the garden, humans are innately sinful, which is why it can be difficult for us to imagine how three divine persons can act in perfect unity with one will. But God is perfectly good, and each divine person shares that same goodness. Their wills are always aligned, with no sin to divide or distort them. <br><br>
So as a follower of Christ, I now see that <strong>God (YHWH) is one in being but tri-personal — Father, Son (Jesus Christ) and Holy Spirit. </strong> And once you read the Bible carefully, you realise something profound: <strong>you don’t have to force the Trinity into the text — the text itself forces you toward the Trinity. </strong> <br><br>
