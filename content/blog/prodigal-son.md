I may have mentioned that I re-subscribed to Audible in previous posts, and at some point I'll have to share a reading list of the audiobooks I've been listening to. This week, however, I wanted to mention <i>The Prodigal God</i> by Timothy Keller.

Funnily enough, it was recommended by Andy Bannister in his audiobook <i>How to Talk About Jesus Without Looking Like an Idiot</i>, another book I would highly recommend.
And yes, <i>The Prodigal God</i> is the correct title. That isn't a typo.

As Keller points out, the word <i>prodigal</i> does not actually mean wayward or rebellious. It refers to spending in a reckless or lavish manner. Keller's argument is that the father in the parable is just as prodigal as the younger son, displaying extravagant generosity towards both of his children.

I'll try to provide a brief summary of the book, although I would strongly encourage you to read it for yourself. Keller has a remarkable ability to illustrate biblical truths in ways that challenge both believers and sceptics alike. Keller draws what I believe is the most compelling conclusion from the parable, that is that throughout our lives, we often become both brothers at different times. Before we get to Keller's central insight, it's worth revisiting the Parable of the Prodigal Son (Luke 15:11–32), a parable notably absent from the Quran.

When people speak about the Prodigal Son, they usually focus exclusively on the younger brother, who rebels against his father, demands his inheritance, leaves home, wastes everything through reckless living, and eventually finds himself broken and destitute. Realising the depth of his failure, he returns home hoping merely to be accepted as a servant. Yet before he can even finish his confession, his father runs to meet him, embraces him, and restores him to the family, holding a feast to celebrate his return.

Yet the parable is not merely about the younger brother, for there are <strong>two lost sons</strong> in this story.

Whilst the younger brother is lost through open rebellion, the elder brother is lost through pride and self-righteousness. On the surface he appears faithful, remaining physically close to his father and obeying his commands. Yet beneath this outward obedience he harbours resentment towards his brother and bitterness towards his father's generosity.

This is precisely why Jesus tells the story. He is addressing both the obvious sinners and the religious leaders of His day, the Pharisees.

The sinners knew they were broken, whilst the Pharisees believed they were righteous through their own works. Yet both groups were separated from the Father's heart, albeit in very different ways.

Keller further highlights a fascinating detail. Under the inheritance customs of the time, the elder brother would receive the larger share of his father's estate. Once the younger son had already received and squandered his inheritance, everything that remained effectively belonged to the elder brother.

The feast, the fattened calf, the robe, the ring, and the celebration all carried a cost. The father's extravagant mercy towards the younger son ultimately came at the expense of what would otherwise have belonged to the elder brother.

Yet despite receiving the greater inheritance, the elder brother refuses to seek his lost sibling, celebrate his return, or share in the joy of his father's mercy. And so, the story concludes with the father pleading for the elder son to come inside and celebrate that his brother, who was once dead, is now alive.

Viewed through this lens, the parable takes on an even deeper significance. Jesus is not simply telling a story about a wayward son returning home. He is inviting the Pharisees to see themselves in the elder brother. Just as the father pleads with the elder son to come inside and celebrate, Jesus is pleading with the religious leaders of His day to rejoice that sinners are being welcomed into the Kingdom of God.

And it is here that Keller's central insight comes into focus. The elder brother should have sought the lost son, shared in his father's joy, and celebrated his return. Instead, he remains outside the feast, angry that grace has been shown to someone he believes does not deserve it.

The parable therefore leaves us searching for a better elder brother. One who seeks the lost, rejoices at their return, and willingly bears the cost of reconciliation.

<strong>For Christians, this points directly to Jesus.</strong>

He left His Father's house and entered the very world that rebelled against Him. He humbled Himself to seek the lost, rejoiced over repentant sinners, bore the cost of reconciliation at the Cross, paid the debt we could never pay, and brought us home to the Father.

## Finding Ourselves in the Story

Finally, we come back to what I believe is Keller's most profound observation. As I mentioned earlier, throughout our lives most of us can identify with both brothers at different times.

The parable is therefore not merely a lesson in theology. It is also a description of the human condition and a challenge to the ways in which religion, irreligion, and our own hearts can distance us from God.

At times, we are like the younger brother. We openly rebel, pursue our own desires, and convince ourselves that life can be found apart from God. In today's context, this may describe those who have left the religious traditions in which they were raised, choosing instead to identify as agnostic, atheist, or simply indifferent to matters of faith.

At other times, we become like the elder brother. Rather than running from God, we attempt to draw near to Him on our own terms. We immerse ourselves in religious activity, work hard, serve faithfully, learn diligently, and strive to obey. Yet beneath it all, we can begin to trust in our own righteousness rather than in God's grace. We subtly believe that our standing before God is determined by our performance rather than His mercy.

What makes the elder brother's attitude particularly dangerous is that it often hides beneath the appearance of faithfulness. When religion becomes pride, judgement, and self-righteousness, it can distort the very message it seeks to proclaim. In doing so, it can even push younger brothers away in the first place, creating the very rebellion it later condemns. The tragedy is that both brothers are separated from the father, but one often fails to recognise that he is lost at all.

The truth is that every one of us carries something of both brothers within us. Whether through open rebellion or self-righteous religion, we all find ways of placing ourselves at the centre rather than God.

## Both Brothers in My Own Story

For me, both brothers have been part of my own story.

And amusingly, I am not referring to my actual brothers, although I do have an older brother who is a devout Muslim and a younger brother who would describe himself more as a cultural Muslim. I love them both dearly and pray that they, like me, would come to know the Lord more deeply and experience a close relationship with Him.

Rather, I mean that I can see both brothers reflected in my own life.

In my younger years, I certainly resembled the younger brother far more than I would like to admit. I pursued my own path and accumulated a great deal of shame through many of my choices. For years I convinced myself that I had no regrets, yet beneath that confidence I knew there were things I wished I could undo. Part of me believed that if I could simply do enough good in the future, somehow, it might outweigh the wrongs of the past.

Later, I began seeking God more seriously and spent time exploring Islam in greater depth. Yet God had other plans for me. Rather than finding the answers I expected, I underwent a profound internal transformation. I was lost, and Christ found me. Indeed, He had always known where I was and precisely when I was ready to be found.

However, becoming a Christian did not remove the temptation to be like the elder brother. If anything, it revealed how deeply those instincts still existed within me.

Although I trust in Christ alone for my salvation, I have often struggled to shake off the legalistic habits that were deeply ingrained in me growing up. I still find myself wanting to measure my standing before God by my performance, as though His love must somehow be earned rather than received. In those moments, I realise that the elder brother within me is never as far away as I would like to think.

## A Continuing Lesson in Grace

In my desire to share the Gospel and see others come to Christ, it is surprisingly easy to begin relying on my own efforts rather than God's grace. I can find myself trusting in knowledge, understanding, correct theology, or my ability to make a persuasive argument. Before long, I am subtly placing my confidence in my own works rather than in what God is doing.

I remember speaking with my younger brother and realising that my desperation for him to come to Jesus could itself become a stumbling block. If I begin acting as though his salvation ultimately depends upon my efforts, my wisdom, or my ability to say the right thing, I risk forgetting that it was never my works that saved me.

It was Christ.

The same Lord who patiently pursued me is capable of pursuing those I love.

Every day I thank the Lord for His mercy because I know I do not deserve it. More importantly, I know there is nothing I could ever do to deserve it. His grace is a gift, not a reward.

And so I pray for my family and friends, trusting that the God who worked in my life can also work in theirs. My responsibility is to love them, pray for them, and point them towards Christ. The saving work itself belongs to Him.

That is why Jesus' words continue to challenge me:

>Why do you see the speck that is in your brother’s eye, 
>but do not notice the log that is in your own eye? 
>Or how can you say to your brother, 
>‘Let me take the speck out of your eye,’ 
>when there is the log in your own eye? 
>You hypocrite, first take the log out of your own eye, 
>and then you will see clearly to take the speck out of your brother’s eye.
> —  Matthew 7:5 (ESV)

We cannot truly know another person's heart. We cannot fully understand their struggles, motives, fears, wounds, or circumstances. We cannot ultimately determine what they deserve.

That knowledge belongs to God alone.

In fact, our actions do not always reveal our motivations. The Pharisees appeared righteous outwardly, yet their hearts were far from God. Likewise, we can appear religious while secretly relying on ourselves rather than on Christ.

The truth is that we all have a log in our eye that prevents us from fully seeing our need for the true Elder Brother, Jesus Christ, and what He has done to reconcile us to God.

## Moral of the Story

Whenever I begin trusting in my own righteousness, I become a little more like the elder brother standing outside the feast.

The Gospel calls me back inside by reminding me that I was once the younger brother, hopelessly lost, and that I become the elder brother whenever I trust in myself. Most importantly, it reminds me that my hope has never rested in either brother.

My hope rests entirely in Jesus Christ, the true Elder Brother, who came to seek the lost, bear the cost of our reconciliation, and bring us home to the Father.

In Him, justice and mercy meet.

And in Him, both lost sons are invited to enter the feast.
