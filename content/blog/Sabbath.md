<strong>When I chose to leave Islam and follow Christ</strong>, change came quickly. One of the clearest signs was that I was finally able to give up smoking and vaping with ease—a habit I had tried, and repeatedly failed, to break since my mid twenties.<br><br>
Like many new believers, I expected clarity, confidence and joy to follow, especially as my eagerness to learn grew. Yet becoming a Christian—though the most profound transformation a person can experience—can also be deeply challenging. Alongside my hunger to learn, I wrestled with doubts, old habits, and the weight of seeing the world through an entirely new lens.<br><br>
As my convictions deepened, my sense of isolation deepened with them. I no longer fitted in at work as I once had, and I often struggled to relate to my non Christian friends, many of whom understandably held the same negative view of Christianity that I once had.<br><br>
I immersed myself in online teachings about the Trinity, difficult passages of Scripture and end times prophecy. Looking back, I can see that not everything I consumed strengthened my understanding in a healthy way. Even so, God used that season to teach me discernment—a lesson I am still learning today.<br><br>

<strong>Learning to Build My Faith on Scripture</strong><br>

Having been burned once before by an institution shaped by human tradition, I was determined not to fall into the same patterns again. My faith had to be grounded in God’s Word, sound doctrine and a clear understanding of Church history.<br><br>
I often felt as though I was piecing my faith together from the ground up, learning everything fresh from Scripture rather than from tradition or upbringing.<br><br>
During this season of searching, I began to notice things in the Bible I had never seen before—particularly surrounding the Sabbath. As I read through Exodus and Deuteronomy, I realised that the Sabbath was not merely an Old Testament custom but part of the Ten Commandments themselves, written by God’s own finger on stone.<br><br>
<i>If the Sabbath is one of the Ten Commandments, why do Christians today no longer keep it? And how did the day of rest move from the seventh day to Sunday?</i><br><br>
I knew I couldn’t simply accept inherited assumptions; I needed to understand it biblically. So, I asked God to guide me as I studied His Word and the history of the early church.<br>

<strong>Rooted in Creation and Command</strong><br>

From the beginning, God blessed the seventh day and set it apart as holy—long before Israel existed.<br><br>
“<i>And on the seventh day God finished his work that he had done, and he rested on the seventh day from all his work that he had done. So God blessed the seventh day and made it holy…</i>” — Genesis 2:2–3 (ESV)<br><br>
The Sabbath was woven into creation itself, not as a burden but as a rhythm of rest. Later, God wrote the Ten Commandments on stone, including the call to “remember the Sabbath day” (Exodus 20:8–11).<br>

<strong>Learning to Study Scripture Carefully</strong><br>

As I examined the verses most often cited against seventh day Sabbath observance—and in support of Sunday worship—I realised how easily early teachings had shaped my thinking.<br><br>
That changed when I learned to test everything through the CAAP method: considering the <strong>Context</strong>, <strong>Author</strong>, <strong>Audience</strong> and <strong>Purpose</strong> of each passage.<br><br>
I began with Colossians 2:<br><br>
“<i>Therefore let no one pass judgment on you… with regard to a festival or a new moon or a Sabbath. These are a shadow of the things to come, but the substance belongs to Christ.</i>” — Colossians 2:16–17 (ESV)<br>

<strong>Colossians 2: Understanding Paul’s Meaning</strong><br>

Paul wrote to a mainly Gentile church influenced by both Jewish tradition and pagan spirituality. Certain teachers insisted that Christians observe dietary rules, festivals, new moons and Sabbaths as part of a system tied to the “elemental spirits of the universe”.<br><br>
The phrase “festivals, new moons and Sabbaths” follows the yearly–monthly–weekly pattern found in Ezekiel 45:17. In Jewish literature, this formula summarised the entire ceremonial calendar, including the sacrificial system that pointed forward to Christ.<br><br>
Even the Sabbath had additional offerings attached to it under the Mosaic law (Numbers 28:9–10).<br><br>
Paul, trained under Gamaliel, almost certainly used this phrase intentionally. He was addressing the <strong>ceremonial system</strong> as a whole—not the weekly Sabbath in isolation.<br><br>
When Paul calls these observances “a shadow of the things to come”, he is speaking about the <strong>ceremonial, sacrificial aspects</strong> of the calendar—not the creation rooted Sabbath rest.<br><br>
The seventh day Sabbath predates sin and stands within the Ten Commandments, so Paul’s reference to “sabbaths” addresses their ritual additions, not the weekly day of rest itself.<br><br>
Paul then warns against spiritual elitism, asceticism and mystical practices that distract from Christ. True growth comes only from holding fast to Him (Colossians 2:18–23).<br><br>
Paul’s teaching in Colossians makes it clear that he is addressing the ceremonial system tied to festivals, new moons and Sabbaths—not the creation rooted rhythm of rest.<br><br>
But Colossians is not the only passage people raise when discussing the Sabbath. <strong>Another verse often referred to is Romans 14:5–6</strong>, where Paul speaks about esteeming one day above another.<br><br>
“<i>One person esteems one day as better than another…</i>” — Romans 14:5–6 (ESV)<br>

<strong>Romans 14: A Different Issue Entirely</strong><br>

Romans 14 addresses tensions between Jewish and Gentile believers over food customs and devotional practices. In verses 5–6, Paul is speaking about <i>fasting days</i>, not the weekly Sabbath.<br><br>
Paul is not redefining the Sabbath or establishing Sunday worship. His concern is unity, humility and love—not calendar reform.<br>

<strong>Historical Considerations</strong><br>

After the apostles, various traditions began shaping Christian practice. Several early writers encouraged Saturday fasting to prevent the Sabbath from being treated as a feast day.<br><br>
Councils such as Laodicea, Hippo and Carthage formalised Saturday fasting and forbade fasting on Sunday—reinforcing Sunday as a day of joy and resurrection.<br><br>
Yet Scripture shows a very different pattern.<br><br>
Paul never attempts to suppress Sabbath rest or promote Sunday observance.<br><br>
Hebrews teaches that the sacrificial system has ceased because Christ’s sacrifice is complete (Hebrews 10:1–14), yet it continues to speak of God’s rest (Hebrews 4:1–11).<br><br>
The Jerusalem Council debated circumcision extensively but said nothing about changing the Sabbath (Acts 15:1–29).<br><br>
The early church met on the first day of the week—but they also met <strong>every day</strong> (Acts 2:46), including the Sabbath (Acts 13:14; Acts 17:2; Acts 18:4).<br><br>
These gatherings were moments of fellowship and teaching, not replacements for the seventh day rest God established at creation.<br><br>
Even the Protestant Reformers faced an unexpected challenge. Catholic apologists argued that Sunday observance rests on <strong>tradition</strong>, not explicit biblical command—whereas the seventh day Sabbath is commanded plainly in Scripture.<br>

<strong>Christ, the Lord of the Sabbath</strong><br>

Jesus kept the Sabbath and declared Himself “Lord of the Sabbath” (Matthew 12:8; Mark 2:28; Luke 6:5).<br><br>
Jesus was buried on the day of Preparation (Matthew 27:62; Mark 15:42; Luke 23:54; John 19:14) and raised on the third day (1 Corinthians 15:3–8).<br><br>
His body rested in the tomb during the Sabbath (Luke 23:56).<br><br>
The women discovered the empty tomb early on the first day of the week (Matthew 28:1; Mark 16:2; Luke 24:1; John 20:1).<br><br>
In this I see a profound fulfilment of the Sabbath pattern: Christ completed the Sabbath rest before the dawn of the first day and the beginning of new creation.<br>

<strong>A Personal Conclusion</strong><br>

For all these reasons, I honour the Sabbath as a voluntary act of faithfulness—a weekly rhythm of rest in Christ—without treating it as a means of salvation or imposing it on others.<br><br>
Christ is the substance; the Sabbath remains a gift. Not something I deserve, nor a way of earning favour, but an expression of love: a quiet rest in the God who saved me by grace through faith and sustains me when I am weak.<br><br>
And with all this said, I recognise that my understanding of Scripture may not be everyone’s interpretation. I am not suggesting that those who do not honour the Sabbath are doing anything wrong.<br><br>
The Christian life is about knowing our identity in Christ, building strong foundations, growing in faith through Scripture, prayer and fellowship with other believers, and navigating life’s challenges with Him at the centre.<br><br>
For me, the Sabbath simply fits within that journey—a gift of rest that helps me delight in the God who calls me His own.<br><br>
