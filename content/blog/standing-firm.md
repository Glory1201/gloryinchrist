Across my recent writings, I have been exploring the foundations of the Christian faith and the challenges believers face in a world full of competing claims. I have examined why Islam’s teachings cannot account for the identity and mission of Jesus, why the Trinity is essential to understanding God as He truly is, why spiritual discernment is necessary in an age of deception, and why sin is far deeper than outward behaviour — a heart problem only Christ can cure. Together, these posts form a single message: we must be rooted in Scripture, clear about who Jesus is, honest about the human condition, and alert to false gospels that distort the truth. Only a faith grounded in God’s Word can stand firm when confusion rises and the world pulls in many directions. <br>

With these truths in view, we can better understand the spiritual climate we are living in today, where Islam appears to be growing quickly and many feel unsettled by the shifting numbers. On the surface, Islam seems to be the fastest growing religion in the world. But much of this growth is driven by demographics — higher birth rates and younger populations — rather than unusually high conversion rates. Research shows that Islam gains only modestly through conversion, while Christianity loses many to secularism in Western countries.<br>

Yet beneath these global trends, a different story is unfolding. In places like Iran, Christianity is growing rapidly despite persecution. Many converts remain officially registered as Muslim because declaring their faith is dangerous. Surveys consistently show that large numbers of Iranians privately identify as Christian or non religious. And in the United States, almost a quarter of Muslims raised in Muslim households no longer identify as Muslim. <br>

We are also living in an age where information is freely available through the internet, allowing people to explore faith with honesty and openness. In that spirit, I want to share a thoughtful documentary that approaches Islam with historical curiosity and an open heart: <a href="https://youtu.be/K2JdTrZO1To?si=KHgScXdzDfIVI_vS"><strong>Islam, The Untold Story – Tom Holland</strong></a><br>

So while Islam may seem to be expanding, the reality is more complex. Beneath the surface, quiet conversions are happening, and the gospel is taking root in places once closed to it. <br>

<strong>The Narrow Gate and the True Gospel</strong><br>

It is a joy to see Muslims coming to Christ, especially in places long closed to the gospel. Yet Jesus reminds us that the way into His kingdom is narrow: <i>“For the gate is narrow and the way is hard that leads to life, and those who find it are few”</i> (Matthew 7:14). So even though Islam may appear to be expanding, the goal of the gospel has never been mass numbers or salvation through human effort. Salvation is God’s gift, freely given to those who believe in the true and living God.<br>

Therefore, I do not believe Islam is the “final boss,” nor the ultimate deception Jesus warned about. Many Muslims are already coming to Christ across the Middle East, and I believe this will continue. My concern — and I include myself here — is that new believers must be firmly grounded in Scripture.<br>

Revelation 17–18 warns about Babylon — a powerful religious political system that deceives nations and persecutes believers. Its promises will look impressive, even spiritual, and those not written in the Book of Life will be drawn to it.<br>

<strong>So what is the Book of Life? </strong><br>

The Book of Life is God’s record of those who belong to Him — a divine register showing who will receive eternal life at the final judgment. Scripture teaches that while the wicked are judged according to their deeds, God’s people are acquitted not because of their works, but because their names are written in the Book of Life. This is a completely different principle from performance based evaluation: inclusion in the Book of Life is rooted in grace, not effort.<br>

Jesus’ promise in Revelation 3:5 carries deep weight: <i>“I will never blot out the name of the one who overcomes”</i>. Early Christians in Sardis (in present day Turkey) understood this vividly. In their world, names could be erased from civic registers before execution — where removal meant death. Christ’s promise of permanence therefore speaks directly into a context where erasure was a real threat. He assures His people that their citizenship in His kingdom is secure and cannot be revoked.<br>

God always preserves His own, even within compromised systems. The true Church is therefore not defined by denominations, but by those who belong to Christ — His sheep, secure in His hand. These are the elect, kept by God’s grace and marked by genuine repentance, obedience, and perseverance.
In Islam, there is no true equivalent to the Book of Life. The closest concepts are the Book of Deeds — a record of every action — and the Book of Destiny, which contains predetermined events. Both operate on the principle of performance. By contrast, Scripture presents the Book of Life as a roster of the righteous — those washed in the blood of the Lamb. The difference is profound: Islam’s heavenly books record actions; Christianity’s Book of Life records identity — those who belong to Christ because He keeps them.<br>

<strong>The Nature of Deception</strong><br>

Throughout history, many movements have claimed to represent Christ while quietly drifting from the true gospel. Scripture suggests that the final deception will not be one single religion, but a broad, inclusive movement that welcomes all beliefs under the banner of unity and peace — while rejecting any faith that refuses to worship the Antichrist. We already see early signs in ideas claiming all religions lead to God or that doctrine no longer matters. And deception rarely appears evil, for Satan “masquerades as an angel of light” (2 Corinthians 11:14). <br>

This need for deep roots becomes even clearer when we consider the challenges of evangelising Muslims wisely and faithfully. Recently I have spent much time studying Scripture and reading books on how to share the gospel with Muslims. One book, <strong>Reaching Your Muslim Neighbor with the Gospel</strong> by A. S. Ibrahim, included a passage that struck me deeply:<br>

<i>“We must be careful theologically to ‘guard the deposit entrusted’ to us… by not mixing, compromising, or distorting the gospel… This is why sound theology is indispensable for proper evangelism.” </i><br>

Ibrahim explains that the stakes are high. Some modern “insider movements” try to reach Muslims by adopting positions that contradict Scripture, which leads to confused converts who hold beliefs the Bible does not support. A loose theological approach can even result in practices like honouring Muhammad as a prophet or encouraging new believers to keep their former religious traditions. This is not a small pastoral issue — it undermines the very gospel these believers are meant to embrace.
What makes Ibrahim’s warning even stronger is his historical perspective. Throughout Christian history, believers refused to honour Muhammad or treat the Quran as inspired, even under persecution. Yet today, some Western evangelicals are willing to compromise long held biblical convictions in the name of cultural relevance.<br>

His warning is clear: Christians must stay rooted in Scripture. When churches soften doctrine to attract people, conversions may look promising at first, but they rarely last. Jesus taught this in the parable of the sower — unless the seed, the Word of God, takes deep root, it will not endure. <br>

<strong>The Parable of the Sower</strong><br>

Since Ibrahim urges us to take a “Christ centred approach” — seeing Islam through Scripture and loving Muslims as people God seeks — it is fitting to turn to Jesus’ teaching. In the parable of the sower (Mark 4:1–20; Luke 8:4–15), He describes four responses to God’s Word. <br>

These patterns still appear today and speak directly to the experience of many converts from Islam, who often face unique pressures, expectations and spiritual challenges as they encounter Christ.<br>

<ul>
<li><strong>The Hardened Path — Closed Hearts:</strong> Some hear the Word, but it never sinks in; Satan snatches it away immediately. This resembles those — whether family members, community leaders, or friends — who refuse to engage with Christian claims at all. The heart is closed before truth can take root.</li><br>

<li><strong>The Rocky Ground — Superficial Faith:</strong> Some receive the Word with joy but fall away when pressure comes. This mirrors Cultural Christianity, the softened version of the gospel many Muslims first encounter. When repentance, discipleship, or Christ’s exclusivity are removed, faith cannot develop depth. Without roots, it collapses under cost — a reality many converts face when family or cultural pressure intensifies.</li><br>

<li><strong>The Thorny Ground — Distracted Faith:</strong> Some hear the Word but are choked by “the cares, riches, and pleasures of life.” Their fruit never matures. This reflects the Prosperity Gospel and other comfort driven versions of Christianity, where competing loyalties slowly suffocate spiritual growth. In multifaith families, this can look like someone who recognises Christ’s truth but feels unable to bear the relational cost of following Him. </li><br>

<li><strong>The Good Soil — Rooted and Persevering: </strong> Those in good soil hear the Word, hold it sincerely, and remain faithful over time. Their endurance is evidence of God’s work in them. Their names are secure in the Book of Life, and their faith strengthens under pressure instead of dying away.</li>

</ul>
My own journey — wrestling with truth, counting the cost, and learning to stand firm — reflects this soil. If anyone tells you that becoming a Christian makes life easier in the conventional, worldly sense, I assure you this is not true. Following Christ changes how you see everything. You are still in the world, but you are no longer of it, and that shift can make ordinary situations feel difficult or even lonely. You begin to step back from things you once freely joined, not out of superiority, but because your heart is being shaped by a different kingdom. Yet I hold onto His Word not because I am strong, but because He is faithful, and He sustains those who belong to Him.<br><br>

<strong>The True Church Beyond Denominations</strong><br>

So, what are we to learn from this parable?  True conversion requires more than initial belief. It demands rootedness, perseverance, and a heart anchored in Scripture. The Church must therefore guard the gospel, refusing to dilute it for comfort or numbers. Softened doctrine cannot produce lasting disciples. Only the unaltered Word of God creates roots deep enough to endure trials, resist deception and bear fruit that lasts. <br>

For this reason, I consider myself non denominational — not because denominations are inherently wrong, but because the Church is deeper and more spiritual than any human organisation. Even so, believers are not meant to walk alone. The early Church gathered, prayed, worshipped and carried one another’s burdens. We are called to do the same. The Church may transcend labels, but we still need a local fellowship where we can grow, serve, be accountable, and walk together in love. <br>

<strong>My Own Journey</strong><br>

Early in my walk, I was briefly drawn toward Catholicism because some of its rituals felt familiar — especially fasting, which resembles Islam’s emphasis on works. But Scripture taught me something vital: obedience flows from love, not from earning favour. <br>

One example is my desire to honour the Sabbath. I keep the Sabbath on Saturdays — or at least try to rest as best I can — because Scripture teaches that if we love God, we will want to keep His commandments (John 14:15). This does not mean Sabbath keeping saves me; salvation is entirely Christ’s work. But love makes me want to set aside a day to worship Him and rest in Him.
Jesus affirmed the Sabbath but challenged legalistic enforcement: <i>“The Sabbath was made for man, not man for the Sabbath”.</i> The Sabbath is a gift — an outward practice of an inward truth: resting in God’s unearned love. Treating Sabbath keeping as if it earns salvation denies the very principle that gives the practice life. <br>

Just to be clear however, I am not a Seventh day Adventist. Though I do believe many sincere believers are within that denomination. My Sabbath practice is simply an expression of love for the one true God — not a requirement for salvation.<br>

I am also not claiming to be right in every detail. My hope is that anyone reading this will be grounded in Scripture and seek guidance from their pastors. We all need the wisdom of mature believers, and we all need the steadying truth of God’s Word.<br>

This is also an area I struggle with personally. Although I have found a good church, my Saturday Sabbath keeping often means I miss attending in person and I end up watching online while Sunday is spent catching up on housework. This is something I hope to work on.<br>

<strong>Rootedness Matters</strong><br>

All of this brings me back to the heart of my message: rootedness matters. Without deep roots in Scripture, people are vulnerable to deception. The Church must guard the gospel and ground new believers in the Word so they can stand firm when trials come and recognise truth when confusion arises. <br>

May He guard our hearts from deception, strengthen us in trials, and draw our families into the light of His salvation. Whatever lies ahead, His grace is enough, His hand is sure, and His promises never fail. <br>
