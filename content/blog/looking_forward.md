<strong>Looking Back, Looking Forward: From Islam to Christ</strong>

It's been a little while since my last post.

I'm not entirely sure whether anyone is reading this website apart from search engines and those I've shared it with directly, but writing has helped me study the Bible more faithfully, and the reach of this blog is ultimately in God's hands.

With that in mind, I'd like to begin a series of shorter weekly reflections. These posts will grow out of my ongoing study of Scripture and my journey from Islam to Christ. Some may touch on Islam and the questions that helped shape my own search for truth, while others will simply explore whatever happens to be on my mind at the time through a biblical lens

To restart, I'd like to revisit some of what I once believed as a Muslim and how those beliefs eventually changed.

<strong>What I Once Believed</strong>

As a Muslim, I believed that Islam preserved the faith of Abraham and that the Quran completed the revelations given through Moses and Jesus. I was also taught that the Bible had been corrupted, which explained why Jews and Christians had departed from God's true path.

At the time, I knew very little about the origins or history of Islam itself. It was only later that I realised how rarely I had examined the foundations of what I believed. Like many others, I had inherited my faith and accepted its claims without seriously exploring the historical, theological, or scriptural evidence behind them.

As I reflected on my upbringing, I also came to understand how closely religion and culture can be intertwined. Some of my fondest childhood memories revolve around Ramadan. I remember waking before dawn for suhoor while my mother quietly rose even earlier to prepare food for the family. It was one of the few times when everyone would be awake together before sunrise.

Those memories remain precious because they remind me of my mother and the time we shared as a family. They also helped me understand why many people feel such a deep attachment to Islam. For many, that attachment is not only to a set of beliefs but also to family, community, tradition, and treasured memories that have become woven together.

For those interested in how that journey unfolded, you may find it helpful to read [My Testimony: From Islam to Christ]( https://gloryinchrist.co.uk/blog/), where I explain in more detail how I came to put my trust in Jesus Christ.

<strong>From Religious Duty to Knowing God</strong>

Fasting provides a good example of how my understanding began to change. In Islam, Ramadan is one of the Five Pillars, and fasting is an obligation for every faithful Muslim.

As I explored Christianity, I realised that fasting serves a different purpose. In Scripture, it is not a prescribed duty but a voluntary act of self-denial that turns our attention towards God. Hunger reminds us that our deepest need is not physical food, but the God who sustains us. The goal is not simply to go without food, but to seek God more earnestly through prayer and dependence on Him.

I found that both profound and beautiful. The more I have grown in my faith, the more I have realised that following Christ is not ultimately about performing religious duties to please God. It is about knowing Him.

This difference between religion as duty and Christianity as a restored relationship with God lies at the heart of the gospel. What once felt like an obligation has become a joy, not because of my own strength, but because the Holy Spirit sustains and transforms me.

>Come to me, all who labor and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest.
>
> Take my yoke upon you, and learn from me, for I am gentle and lowly in heart,
> and you will find rest for your souls.
>
> For my yoke is easy, and my burden is light.
>
> — Matthew 11:28–30 (ESV)

<strong>Who This Blog Is For</strong>

So, I guess the point I am making is that Islam often functions as a cultural and communal identity as much as a religion. That was certainly true of me for much of my life, and it is one reason I began examining my beliefs more carefully.

In many ways, this blog is written for people like I once was: cultural Muslims who genuinely want to seek the truth wherever it leads. It is also written for Christians who want to understand and engage with Muslims thoughtfully, sharing the gospel with grace, humility and a sincere desire to point others to Christ.

<strong>How Do We Come to Know God?</strong>

As Christians, we are called to proclaim the gospel and to show the world what it means to follow Christ. And to do this we should desire to grow in our knowledge of God through Scripture, prayer, repentance and obedience to His commands. My article, [Standing Firm in Truth: A Call to Rooted Faith in an Age of Deception]( https://gloryinchrist.co.uk/blog/standing-firm/) on my understanding of how Christians can stay deeply rooted in Scripture, and love their neighbour by faithfully proclaiming the unaltered gospel and persevere in Christ regardless of cultural pressure, persecution, or theological compromise.

After all, when Jesus was asked which commandment was the greatest, He replied:

> "The most important is, 'Hear, O Israel: The Lord our God, the Lord is one.
> And you shall love the Lord your God with all your heart and with all your soul
> and with all your mind and with all your strength.'
>
> The second is this:
>
> 'You shall love your neighbour as yourself.'
>
> There is no other commandment greater than these."
>
> — Mark 12:29–31 (ESV)

If you're a Muslim, you may be thinking right now, "Wait, that's a verse where Jesus says that God is one." And I completely agree with you. In fact, Christians also believe that there is only one God.

One of the biggest misunderstandings many Muslims have about Christianity is that Christians believe in three gods. That is not the case. My article, [“Understanding the Trinity and Why it matters”]( https://gloryinchrist.co.uk/blog/trinity) explores in greater detail how Christians believe that God is one Being who exists eternally as three distinct Persons: the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit. It also explains how this understanding is progressively revealed throughout Scripture and why this truth is central to the Christian understanding of salvation, worship and God's relationship with humanity.

Earlier I mentioned that following Christ is ultimately about knowing God rather than merely performing religious duties. But how can we love someone we do not truly know? God has revealed Himself through the Scriptures, and the more we seek Him there, the more we come to understand His character, His purposes, and His will. As our knowledge of Him grows, so too does our love for Him.

As Proverbs reminds us:

> For the Lord gives wisdom;
> from his mouth come knowledge and understanding.
>
> — Proverbs 2:6 (ESV)

True wisdom does not come merely from tradition, culture or human effort. It comes from God Himself. As we seek Him through His Word, He grants understanding and opens our eyes to the truth.

I know this because I once believed I could see, yet I was blind. But through His grace, God opened my eyes to who Christ really is.

> For judgment I came into this world, that those who do not see may see,
> and those who see may become blind.
>
> — John 9:39 (ESV)

But understanding who Christ is naturally raises another question: Why did He come? The answer lies in humanity's problem of sin and God's provision of atonement. This was one of the most significant areas where my understanding of Christianity and Islam began to diverge.

<strong>Why the Question of Sin and Atonement Matters</strong>

If you have never considered the biblical teaching on the fall of humanity and original sin, I would encourage you to read my article [Why Good Deeds Cannot Save Us: The Real Problem of Sin](https://gloryinchrist.co.uk/blog/sin/) before continuing. It provides the foundation for much of what follows. 

This is particularly important for Muslim readers, since the Christian understanding of sin differs significantly from the Islamic perspective, so provides an important insight into these differences.

I remember watching a discussion at Speakers' Corner between a Christian and a Muslim on this very subject. The Muslim rejected the idea that sin requires atonement, arguing that God can simply forgive those who repent.

What struck me later is that modern Rabbinic Judaism has largely arrived at a similar conclusion. Following the destruction of the Second Temple in AD 70, the sacrificial system prescribed in the Law of Moses could no longer be practised. As a result, the emphasis shifted towards repentance, prayer and good deeds rather than sacrifices for atonement.

Yet when we turn to the Hebrew Scriptures themselves, sacrifice appears to play a central role in God's provision for sin. As Leviticus states:

> For the life of the flesh is in the blood, and I have given it for you
> on the altar to make atonement for your souls, for it is the blood that
> makes atonement by the life.
>
> — Leviticus 17:11 (ESV)

Even more intriguing is a passage found in the [Talmud (Yoma 39b)](https://www.sefaria.org/Yoma.39b?lang=bi), which records that for forty years before the destruction of the Temple, certain signs associated with God's acceptance of the sacrifices ceased to occur, including the scarlet thread no longer turning white. Christians have often noted that this period roughly coincides with the time of Jesus' crucifixion and resurrection.

While the Talmud itself does not connect these events to Christ, I found it noteworthy that the very period in which Christians believe the ultimate sacrifice for sin was offered is also remembered in Jewish tradition as a time when the signs associated with the Temple sacrifices apparently ceased.

For me, the question eventually became unavoidable: if God established a sacrificial system to deal with sin, and that system has now been absent for nearly two thousand years, how is sin dealt with today?

Christianity answered that question in a way neither Islam nor modern Rabbinic Judaism could. Christ did not abolish the need for atonement. He fulfilled it.

<strong>Questions That Led Me to Christ</strong>

Islam teaches that repentance is sufficient for the forgiveness of sins, yet sacrifice remains deeply embedded in Islamic tradition through Eid al-Adha. This raised an obvious question for me: if God established a sacrificial system, what purpose did it serve?

That question forced me to look more carefully at what Scripture teaches about sin, sacrifice and atonement. Is sin simply a matter of wrongdoing that can be forgiven through repentance alone, or does it require God Himself to provide a remedy?

The answer cannot be understood apart from the Bible's larger story of redemption. If that theme is unfamiliar, you may find it helpful to read [Why I Believe the Bible Tells the True Story of the World](https://gloryinchrist.co.uk/blog/divine_council/).

Having come to believe that God has revealed Himself through history as well as through Scripture, I think it would be worthwhile to explore the historical evidence for the Christian faith in greater depth and to compare it with the historical foundations of Islam.

Ultimately, my aim is not merely to critique another faith, but to encourage readers to honestly consider the evidence and, above all, the claims of Jesus Christ Himself.

For now, I hope this testimony has encouraged you to seek God through His Word, to examine the evidence for yourself, and to remain open to where the truth may lead.

My prayer for every reader is that God would bless you, keep you, reveal Himself to you through His Word and lead you into the knowledge of His Son, Jesus Christ. May you know His grace, His truth, and His peace.  – Inspired by Numbers 6:24–26)
